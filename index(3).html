This is why the loop counts every backtest it ever runs. The bar a strategy must clear (a deflated Sharpe ratio) rises with the number of tries, so hypothesis 40 has to beat a higher bar than hypothesis 1.

The simulation runs live in your browser: 504 days of random returns per strategy, Sharpe ratios annualised. The "luck" line is the expected best of that many tries with no skill at all.

The survivor, H9, on 17 months it had never seen

H9 was frozen during the first loop, before the holdout existed for it. Then it ran on January 2025 to May 2026, a stretch where bitcoin lost a fifth of its value.

What persuades me isn't one big number. It's that the excess Sharpe sits around 1.1 to 1.2 in all three eras. H9 also had a −0.24 correlation with bitcoin and was invested only lightly on average, sitting in cash much of the time.

Promising, not proven

On 17 months alone, a Sharpe of 1.06 is about a one-sigma result. Across all six years it's closer to three sigma.

Two big days did a lot of work

Most of the holdout gain came from two crash events. A strategy that wins on rare days needs a long live record before anyone should trust it.

Still a backtest

The next test nobody can game is the future: H9 is being built into a paper-trading bot with a kill switch and hard position limits.

What went wrong, and what changed because of it

An honest research loop is mostly a record of mistakes caught. These are the ones that shaped the design.

The first bot was too ambitious

Supercluster had three agents (MELCHIOR-1, BALTHASAR-2 and CASPER-3) voting on trades. It passed walk-forward checks (efficiency 0.63) but missed my own 70% Monte Carlo bar, so it never went live.

Narrower questions, one at a time.

A "new" strategy was the old one in disguise

One early candidate was 0.98 correlated with the trend-following baseline it was meant to beat.

Novel now means under 0.5 correlation with everything before it.

Patterns without reasons flipped

Calendar and macro ideas found by pattern-hunting reversed out of sample.

Every hypothesis starts from an economic reason the edge should exist.

Gate results leaked into later ideas

In the second loop, a failed gate's post-mortem directly shaped the next hypothesis. Each gate was run once, but its answer still steered the search.

The next loop freezes all candidates first, then gates them in one sealed batch.

The backtester changed mid-loop

The engine was edited between gate runs, so early and late ideas may not have been scored by the same rules.

Engine changes are locked and diffed between laps.

The second loop found nothing

No hypothesis cleared the bar. That's an honest null, and the setup is built to report one rather than tweak until something passes.

A null is a result, not a failure.
